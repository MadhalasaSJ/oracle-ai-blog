01
The problem

Raw tables don't tell you what needs attention right now

Any active supply chain generates a lot of data, and it's spread across a lot of different places. Suppliers, products, warehouses, inventory counts, purchase orders, shipments, all of it usually sits in its own table. Each table can answer a narrow question on its own. But none of them, by itself, tells you how the supply chain is actually doing right now.

Think about the kind of questions a supply chain manager asks on a normal day. How much inventory do we actually have right now? Which products are close to running out and need to be reordered? Are any shipments delayed, and where? Which suppliers are risky enough that we should be watching them more closely? What's the total value sitting in purchase orders that are still pending?

None of these have a clean answer if you have to open five different tables, run a query for each one, and then try to hold all of it in your head. That's not really a data problem, it's a visibility problem. The information already exists, it's just scattered, and scattered information is slow to act on.

That gap, between the data existing somewhere and someone being able to act on it in ten seconds, is what I wanted to close with this project.

02
The solution

SupplySense AI: one dashboard, the whole supply chain

SupplySense AI is the Supply Chain Control Center I ended up building, using Oracle APEX on top of an Oracle Database. The idea behind it is fairly simple. Pull the most important supply chain information onto a single page, so a manager can look at one screen and understand what's going on instead of opening a dozen different reports.

It covers inventory, suppliers, warehouses, purchase orders and shipments. In practice that means it helps with spotting products that need to be reordered, catching delayed shipments early, understanding which suppliers carry more risk, and keeping an eye on how much money is tied up in purchase orders at any given stage.

Instead of making someone go inspect raw records, SupplySense AI shows that same information as KPIs, charts, alerts and a calendar. The important parts surface on their own, you don't have to go dig for them.

04
How Oracle APEX was used

APEX built the interface, SQL did the thinking

I didn't want to build a separate backend, a separate API layer, and then a separate frontend on top of all that. Oracle APEX let me skip most of it and build the application directly on top of the Oracle database, using the same SQL that already describes the data.

Roughly, this is how I think about the split between the three layers:

DATABASE
Stores the raw supply chain records: products, suppliers, inventory, orders, shipments.
↓
SQL
Filters, joins, groups and aggregates that data into something meaningful, and applies the actual business logic (which suppliers are high risk, which products are below safety stock, and so on).
↓
APEX
Takes the output of those SQL queries and turns it into cards, charts, reports, activity views and a calendar, wrapped in an actual navigable application.

Every card, chart and report on the dashboard is a region powered by a SQL query. There's no separate layer translating the data before it reaches the screen. The SQL is where the real logic lives. APEX is what makes that logic visible and usable.

05
Dashboard walkthrough

Walking through what's actually on the screen

Here's a look at the main pieces of the dashboard, and what each one is actually doing.

KPI

Supply chain KPI cards

Products, suppliers, warehouses, purchase orders, total inventory and delayed shipments, shown as a quick summary right at the top. It answers the "what's the overall state of things" question before you look at anything else.

WH

Inventory by warehouse

A chart comparing how much inventory each warehouse is holding. It's an easy way to see which locations are carrying more stock and which ones are running lighter.

SH

Shipment status

A breakdown of shipments across delivered, processing, in transit and delayed. If delayed shipments spike, you notice it right away instead of it hiding inside individual records.

SR

Supplier risk overview

Suppliers grouped into low, medium and high risk. It gives a fast read on whether the supplier base is leaning risky at any given moment.

PO

Purchase order status

Purchase orders grouped by status, paired with cards showing the count and total value for each one. So you get both the shape of it and the actual money involved.

!

Inventory alerts

This compares current stock against safety stock and flags anything that's fallen below it with a reorder badge. As an example, current stock at 61 against a safety stock requirement of 121 would get flagged.

SA

Supplier risk activity

A compact view that combines a supplier's status, risk level, rating and lead time in one place. It makes it easy to spot a supplier that's high risk, poorly rated and slow, all at the same time.

CAL

Shipment delivery calendar

A calendar view of upcoming deliveries by date. It shifts the dashboard from just describing what's happening to also showing when it's happening.

Below all of this, there are also detailed report sections for recent shipments and recent purchase orders, along with a side menu leading into individual pages for suppliers, products, warehouses, inventory and more. The dashboard works as the summary, and the rest of the application is where you actually go to dig in.

06
Techniques used

What's actually happening under "I used APEX"

Saying "I used Oracle APEX" doesn't explain much by itself, so here's what that actually meant for this project.

SQL queries with joins across tables GROUP BY and aggregate functions Oracle database tables and relationships APEX Cards regions APEX Chart regions (bar, donut) APEX Calendar region APEX Report / classic report regions Page regions and page navigation Side navigation menu Badges and status indicators Avatars / icons Dashboard grid layout SQL-driven dynamic content

A couple of examples of the actual logic behind these. The reorder alert compares current stock against safety stock directly in SQL, if current stock is less than or equal to safety stock, the product gets flagged. Supplier risk comes from grouping suppliers into low, medium and high categories and counting them. Purchase order value by status is just aggregating the total amount for every order in each status. None of it is decorative, every chart and card on the page is the output of a query actually doing work.

09
Oracle technology & learning

What I took away from building this

This project pushed me past just writing individual queries or building a single report. What I actually learned was how the three layers work together as one thing. Oracle Database holds the structured data. SQL is where that data turns into actual business logic, deciding what counts as a risk, what counts as low stock, what counts as a delay. Oracle APEX takes those SQL results and turns them into something a person can actually look at and use, without needing a separate frontend built from scratch.

The other thing that became clear while building this was that a dashboard works better when it's layered. KPI cards give the overview, charts give the patterns, alerts and activity views point at what needs attention, and detailed reports and the calendar give the specifics. Someone who just wants a ten-second glance and someone who wants to dig into the actual records both end up served by the same page.

Right now, SupplySense AI is mainly about visibility and monitoring, showing what's happening and flagging what needs attention. Where I'd like to take it next is toward things like demand forecasting, predicting which suppliers or shipments are likely to run into trouble, automated reorder suggestions, and maybe eventually letting someone ask the dashboard a question in plain language instead of navigating to a chart. That would move it from a dashboard that describes the supply chain to one that actually helps plan around it.
